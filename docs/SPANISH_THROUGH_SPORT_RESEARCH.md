# Learning Spanish through football and F1

Research and proposed ArenaVoca learning experiments, 13 October 2026.

ArenaVoca should connect three experiences: watching a sport the learner understands, exploring a short story at an accessible language level, and recalling useful Spanish later. Football and F1 stories can bridge the time between events and broaden the language beyond action labels. The first outcome to target is understanding and using a few phrases in a new situation, rather than completing a large card collection.

The evidence below supports components of that approach. It does not establish that ArenaVoca, sports fandom or VR improves Spanish learning. The activities and timings are design proposals to test; the proposed participant studies have not been run.

## What the research supports

| Research | Finding and scope | Implication for ArenaVoca |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sutton and Webb, 2026, [audiovisual input meta-analysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-audiovisual-input-on-second-language-learning-a-metaanalysis/9B61BAEF14F110F01148E398D171634A) | Synthesised 56 experiments involving 1,954 learners using pre/post comparisons. Learning gains were reported; educational video categories had larger effects than entertainment categories. These comparisons do not establish that adding our cards causes an improvement. | Treat the broadcast as a source of meaningful situations. Add deliberate learning support and measure its contribution separately. |
| Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate and Desmet, 2013, [captioned video meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2013.07.013) | Across 18 studies, captions in the language being learned benefited listening comprehension and vocabulary. This does not establish that two complete subtitle tracks are always better. | Offer Spanish captions with short, optional native-language help. Let learners reduce support as comprehension improves. |
| Peters and Webb, 2018, [vocabulary through television](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/incidental-vocabulary-acquisition-through-viewing-l2-television-and-factors-that-affect-learning/0E45A630F37C48A5BDB6CC3F725ADDC9) | Two experiments with Dutch-speaking learners of English found incidental meaning learning. Frequency, existing vocabulary knowledge and cognateness affected learning. | Match the language to what a learner already knows and revisit selected phrases. Watching alone should not mark a phrase as learned. |
| Webb and Chang, 2015, [graded reading with audio](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168814559800) | In a study of 61 learners, reading and listening to multiple graded readers produced vocabulary gains assessed immediately and later. Frequency/distribution correlations were not significant in that study. This was a sustained programme, not a two-minute story test. | Try connected, accessible sports episodes over time. Our short-story format remains a hypothesis, and repetition counts alone are insufficient. |
| Karpicke and Roediger, 2008, [retrieval practice](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2008/2008_Karpicke_Roediger_Science.pdf) | Repeated retrieval of foreign-language word pairs improved week-delayed recall after initial success; further study alone did not have the same effect in this experiment. | Ask learners to recall before revealing an answer. Revisit successful phrases later as well as correcting mistakes. |
| Nakata and Suzuki, 2019, [spacing and related vocabulary](https://waseda.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effects-of-massing-and-spacing-on-the-learning-of-semantically-re/) | Studied 133 learners with English–Japanese word pairs. Related words caused more interference errors; spacing benefits differed by item relatedness. | Introduce a small number of clearly differentiated phrases. Space practice and monitor confusion rather than showing every near-synonym together. |
| Makransky, Terkildsen and Mayer, 2019, [VR and cognitive load](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/adding-immersive-virtual-reality-to-a-science-lab-simulation-caus/) | In a science simulation with 52 university students, immersive VR increased presence but reduced learning relative to desktop and increased measured cognitive load. This is a caution from another subject, not a verdict on sports language learning. | Preserve the match as the main focus. Make learning optional, quiet and easy to defer. Measure enjoyment and missed action alongside recall. |

Nation's [Four Strands](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2007-Four-strands.pdf) offers a useful curriculum framework: understanding meaningful input, producing meaningful output, deliberate language study and developing fluency with familiar material. ArenaVoca can cover these through viewing, short conversations, focused practice and retelling. The framework does not require dividing each match into four equal lessons.

## A proposed learning journey

The following durations and phrase counts are starting settings for usability tests, not research-established optima.

| Moment | Learner experience | Football example | F1 example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before viewing, about one minute | Choose a language-support level and meet three useful phrases. | pasa el balón; queda un minuto; necesitamos un gol | entra en boxes; cambia los neumáticos; vuelve a la pista |
| During viewing | A discreet optional card connects one confirmed event to a phrase and illustration. No compulsory response. | A pass becomes “Pasa el balón.” | A pit entry becomes “Entra en boxes.” |
| When the learner chooses, or after viewing | Open a short illustrated story reusing those phrases. | One final attack with little time left. | A driver decides when to change tyres. |
| After the story, about one minute | Recall one phrase, complete one sentence, explain one choice. | “Queda un ___. Necesitamos un gol.” | “El piloto entra en ___.” |
| On a later day, about two minutes | Recall the phrase with a different image, then use it in a short exchange. | Describe a different pass; say what the team needs. | Describe a different pit entry; say what happens next. |

Test next-day and several-days-later practice as an initial schedule, adapting after mistakes. The exact best interval depends on the learner and the retention goal.

The viewer can begin with native-language commentary and optional Spanish phrases. A later step introduces short, supported Spanish listening passages; a further step uses Spanish commentary with Spanish captions and translation on request. Never switch automatically or imply that Spanish commentary is available when the source has no such track. Prepared story narration can supply Spanish practice independently of the broadcast.

Opening, closing or completing any activity must not pause, seek, dim or resume the match. The chosen card stays pinned. A race under a safety car, a replay or a football stoppage may still be important viewing: offer a saved story without automatically starting it. Narration starts only by choice; two competing speech streams should not be the default.

## What stories add

Stories can address two gaps: useful practice between fixtures and the gap between knowing sports nouns and holding a conversation. They also support literal gap-fill exercises, provided learners first understand the situation.

| Story theme | Football treatment | F1 treatment | Language beyond sport |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A comeback | A team needs one more goal. | A driver works through the field. | todavía; queda; necesitar; no rendirse |
| A decision | Pass or shoot? | Stop now or continue? | prefiero; creo que; porque; ahora |
| A sequence | Build-up, pass, shot, save. | Pit entry, tyre change, return. | primero; después; antes de; al final |
| A setback | A missed chance and another attempt. | A slow stop and recovery. | un problema; otra vez; ayudar |
| A fan's day | Meeting friends and finding a seat. | Travelling to the circuit and finding a grandstand. | dónde; cuánto; a qué hora; quiero |
| A personal journey | A fictional player joins a new team. | A fictional mechanic's first race weekend. | presentarse; trabajar; aprender; sentirse |

These themes are proposed ways to connect learner interest with useful language; sporting interest itself is not proof of better retention.

Keep three content types visibly distinct. A **moment from your match** must link to the recording and timestamp. A **historical sports story** needs verified sources and a spoiler indication. A **fictional practice story** can offer choices and invented dialogue, but must not be presented as something a real player or driver said.

Two grounded historical candidates are Barça's 6–1 win over PSG on 8 March 2017, which completed a 6–5 aggregate comeback, and Alonso's 2005 San Marino victory, with Schumacher finishing 0.215 seconds behind. These offer themes of time, persistence, comparison and sequence. The factual starting points are the [club's match report](https://www.fcbarcelona.com/en/news/775237/match-report-fc-barcelona-61-psg-miracle-makers-65-aggregate/amp) and [official F1 result](https://www.formula1.com/en/results/2005/races/774/sanmarino/race-result). Retell facts in original language; do not invent private thoughts or team radio. These are separate historical lessons, not claims about the user's current recording.

## Two concrete lesson prototypes

Both examples below are original fictional practice stories. They are designed for supported beginner practice, not certified CEFR assessments. Spanish wording and eventual narration need fluent review. Each story can use three illustrated panels, phrase-level translation on demand and optional narration. The same visual style should carry across both sports.

### Football lesson about the final attack

Learning aim: recognise and use **queda un minuto**, **pasa el balón** and **necesitamos un gol**.

Panel one: “Queda un minuto. El equipo azul pierde por un gol. Necesitamos un gol para empatar.”

Meaning: One minute remains. The blue team is one goal behind. We need a goal to equalise.

Panel two: “Ana tiene el balón. Ve a Leo cerca de la portería. Ana pasa el balón a Leo.”

Meaning: Ana has the ball. She sees Leo near the goal. Ana passes the ball to Leo.

Panel three: “Leo dispara. El balón entra. ¡Gol! Ahora el partido está empatado.”

Meaning: Leo shoots. The ball goes in. Goal! Now the match is level.

Practice starts with a meaning question: “¿Por qué necesita un gol el equipo?” Offer “Para empatar” and a plausible contrasting meaning, with native-language help available. Then show “Ana ___ el balón a Leo” without immediately revealing the answer. Beginners may request a word bank; record that as supported recognition rather than unaided recall. Answer: **pasa**.

For transfer, show two different players in a new position and ask the learner to describe the pass. Finish with a short exchange: “¿Qué necesita tu equipo?” The response “Necesitamos un gol” practises a useful chunk. Later, “Necesitamos ayuda” extends the same structure beyond football.

### F1 lesson about a pit stop

Learning aim: recognise and use **entra en boxes**, **cambia los neumáticos** and **vuelve a la pista**.

Panel one: “El piloto sigue en la pista. Los neumáticos están gastados. Decide parar ahora.”

Meaning: The driver is still on track. The tyres are worn. The driver decides to stop now.

Panel two: “El piloto entra en boxes. El equipo cambia los neumáticos. Todos trabajan juntos.”

Meaning: The driver enters the pits. The team changes the tyres. Everyone works together.

Panel three: “El piloto vuelve a la pista. Tiene neumáticos nuevos. La carrera continúa.”

Meaning: The driver returns to the track. The driver has new tyres. The race continues.

Ask “¿Qué cambia el equipo?” before a gap fill: “El piloto entra en ___.” Answers: **los neumáticos** and **boxes**. Follow with an ordering task using the three illustrated events. Then invite “Primero entra en boxes. Después vuelve a la pista.”

An optional decision branch asks “¿Prefieres parar ahora o después?” Accept either preference when the Spanish conveys it; do not score an opinion as a racing-strategy fact. Explain any simulated outcome as fictional. Later, use “Ahora o después” in an everyday scheduling exchange.

## Progression beyond picture quizzes

Begin with recognising the meaning of a spoken or written phrase, then move to a gap fill with hints, unaided recall, a new pictured situation, and a short response to another person. These are different abilities and should have separate progress evidence. A correct picture choice does not establish speaking ability.

Keep early language concrete and supported. Introduce sequencing and simple reasons next; reserve retelling in the past and discussing alternatives for learners ready for them. A1/A2/B1 can guide editorial planning using the [Council of Europe's descriptors](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/cefr-descriptors), but card completion should not award those levels.

Use simple scenes with clear contrasts in the same art style. Test new pictures during later recall so the learner cannot succeed only by remembering a card's colour. Avoid introducing several nearly interchangeable technical terms at once. For speech, offer a reviewed example and a chance to try again; speech recognition alone cannot justify a pronunciation score.

## How generated content should work

The AI first needs a supported event, not just a visually plausible image. A recording timestamp, aligned commentary, nearby frames and available event data establish the candidate moment. Commentary may discuss a replay or earlier action. Uncertain moments should remain unpublished.

From the confirmed moment and the learner's chosen level, propose three target phrases and a short story. Reuse known language deliberately, introduce only a small amount of new vocabulary, and retain sources for factual claims. Generate illustrations from an approved scene description and a shared style reference. Review whether the image actually shows the intended action; a convincing car image may still depict pit exit instead of pit entry.

Store the story's provenance, target phrases, translations, review status, spoiler boundary, question answers and accepted response variants alongside it. Separate source facts from fictional teaching branches. Check factual grounding, language quality and answerability before making the pack available. Generation can happen ahead of a recorded viewing session or in the background; playback stays independent. Existing models are appropriate for the first experiment, with reviewed packs as a reliable fallback.

## Studies to run with ArenaVoca

### Study one on comfort and comprehension

Invite 6–8 adult Spanish beginners or improvers, with football and F1 interests represented, for an exploratory prototype study. Observe a short viewing session, one optional card, one story and a recap. Check whether participants understand each illustration, can defer learning, notice important match events and can explain what they think they learned. Ask about effort and enjoyment separately. Record actual errors and missed controls rather than relying only on preference ratings. This small study finds usability problems; it cannot demonstrate general learning effectiveness.

### Study two on stories and delayed recall

Use a small initial pilot of around 12–20 learners to test the study procedure, not to make a powered efficacy claim. Compare match plus cards with match plus cards plus a connected story, while matching total learning time and planned target-phrase exposures. The comparison condition therefore receives equivalent additional card practice. Use matched phrase sets, counterbalance order and content across learners, and record prior Spanish and sport knowledge. Test one sport first to avoid confusing a sport difference with a treatment effect.

Pretest target knowledge, then measure immediate understanding and recall after about 48 hours and one week. Test recall before recognition so answer options do not cue the response. Include a new image or sentence, an optional short spoken response scored by a fluent reviewer, perceived effort, enjoyment and missed match events. With this pilot size, report individual results, uncertainty and feasibility. A larger study would need a sample-size calculation based on a meaningful target difference.

### Study three on optional prompts during viewing

Compare sparse optional in-match cues with end-of-segment invitations using matched clips, counterbalanced order and the same learning content. Never force a pause in either condition. Measure uptake, ignored cues, recall, reported distraction and event comprehension. This tests the scheduling hypothesis directly: uninterrupted playback alone does not establish uninterrupted attention.

## Recommended first build

Build one verified football moment, one three-panel story and a three-step recap: recognise, recall, use. Let the learner save that story for mobile. Then apply the same format to one F1 moment to check that the learning model generalises across sports.

For the event demo, prepare and review the pack in advance and label it accordingly. The proposed narrative is concrete: the viewer sees a pass, understands it in Spanish, meets the phrase in a story, and can use it again the next day. Automatic ingestion, new story generation and improved learning remain separate features or hypotheses until demonstrated.
